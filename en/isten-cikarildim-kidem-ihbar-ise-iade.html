Dismissed From Your Job? What You Need to Know to Protect Your Severance, Notice and Reinstatement Rights

4 June 2026

The decisions a dismissed employee makes in the first few days often affect the amount of compensation they will receive more than the lawsuit conducted in the following months. Signing a document placed in front of them, missing a one-month deadline or agreeing too quickly at a mediation meeting can cause losses that cannot be undone.

In this article we set out, under current Turkish legislation, the key points an employee whose contract has been terminated by the employer should know about severance and notice pay, reinstatement, other employment receivables, mandatory mediation and limitation periods.

1. First step: how was the contract terminated?

The first question that determines compensation rights is on what grounds and how the contract was terminated. The employer must notify the termination in writing and state the reason clearly and precisely. The reason given in the termination notice, the exit code reported to the Social Security Institution (SGK) and the statements of witnesses at the workplace are the most important evidence later on, both for the employer and for the employee.

Terminations based on just cause, particularly "conduct contrary to morality and good faith", may deprive the employee of both severance and notice pay and unemployment benefit. If the reason given by the employer is believed to be untrue, this should be documented before the evidence is lost.

2. Severance pay

Under Article 14 of Law No. 1475, which remains in force, an employee who has worked for at least one year is entitled to severance pay if the contract is terminated by the employer for any reason other than just cause (conduct contrary to morality and good faith). Military service, retirement, a female employee leaving within one year of marriage, termination by the employee for just cause and death also give rise to severance pay.

Severance pay is calculated on the basis of thirty days' wages for each full year of service; periods exceeding a year are taken into account at the same rate. The calculation is based not on the bare wage but on the "gross inclusive" wage, including continuous benefits such as meals, transport, regular bonuses and premiums. The annual amount may not exceed the severance pay ceiling, which is set every six months.

3. Notice pay

In indefinite-term employment contracts, notice must be given to the other party before termination. Under Article 17 of the Labour Law, this period is two, four, six or eight weeks depending on the employee's length of service. If the employer terminates the contract without observing this period or without paying the wages for the notice period in advance, it must pay notice compensation equal to the wages for the notice period.

If the contract of an employee outside the scope of job security is terminated through abuse of the right of termination, the employee is also entitled to bad-faith compensation equal to three times the notice period.

4. Reinstatement: the one-month limitation

An employer that terminates the indefinite-term contract of an employee with at least six months' service at a workplace employing thirty or more employees must rely on a valid reason (Labour Law Article 18). The burden of proving that the termination was based on a valid reason lies with the employer.

An employee who believes that no reason was given in the termination notice, or that the reason given is not valid, must apply to a mediator with a reinstatement claim within one month of the notification of termination. This is a strict time limit; once missed, the right to reinstatement is lost. If no agreement is reached at mediation, a lawsuit may be filed in the labour court within two weeks of the date of the final mediation report.

If the case is won and the employer does not reinstate the employee within one month, the employer must pay compensation for non-reinstatement equal to four to eight months' wages, plus wages and other entitlements for up to four months of the period spent out of work. The choice between a reinstatement claim and a claim for severance and notice pay should be made strategically, depending on whether the employee genuinely wants to return to work and on the strength of the evidence.

5. Other receivables that are often overlooked

Severance and notice pay are often only part of the claims. Other items most frequently claimed in practice are:

  • Pay for unused annual leave,
  • Overtime pay for work exceeding forty-five hours per week,
  • Pay for work performed on national and public holidays,
  • Unpaid salary, premiums and bonuses,
  • Periods of employment that were under-reported or not reported to social security at all.

Payroll records, bank records, entry and exit logs, correspondence and witness statements are assessed together in proving these claims. Preserving records that are accessible before leaving the job directly affects the course of the case.

6. Mandatory mediation

Under Article 3 of Labour Courts Law No. 7036, applying to a mediator is a precondition for lawsuits concerning employee receivables, compensation and reinstatement. The mediator generally concludes the application within three weeks; this period may be extended by one week where necessary. During mediation, limitation periods are suspended and statutory time limits do not run.

A party who fails to attend the first meeting without a valid excuse is held liable for half of the other party's litigation costs even if it later wins the case, and any attorney's fee awarded in its favour is halved. An agreement signed at mediation has the force of a court judgment; no lawsuit can later be filed on the matters agreed. It is therefore essential to check, before signing, which receivables the report covers and whether the amounts have been calculated correctly.

7. Release documents and limitation periods

Documents stating "I have received all my rights" signed when leaving a job are not always valid. Under Article 420 of the Turkish Code of Obligations, a release agreement concerning an employee's receivables must be made in writing at least one month after the end of the contract, must clearly specify the type and amount of the receivable, and payment must be made in the amount due through a bank. Release documents that do not meet these conditions do not extinguish the employee's rights.

The limitation period for severance pay, notice pay, bad-faith compensation, annual leave pay and wage receivables is five years. The one-month period for reinstatement, by contrast, is a strict time limit that is entirely different from limitation.

8. Conclusion

Although a dismissed employee's rights are clearly set out in law, protecting them depends on attention to deadlines and documents. The content of the termination notice, the exit code, the documents signed and the approach taken at mediation are the main factors that determine the compensation received. Taking the right step within the one-month period for reinstatement in particular prevents losses that cannot be remedied later.
