Objecting to a Detention Order in Turkey: Which Routes to Release Are Open?

6 August 2026

Families who learn that a relative has been detained on remand usually ask the same questions: Why was the decision made, when will they be released, and will an objection change anything? Pre-trial detention is not a punishment but an exceptional protective measure applied while proceedings are ongoing. For this reason the law subjects detention to strict conditions and provides more than one avenue for the decision to be reviewed at every stage.

In this article we summarise, under current legislation, the conditions a detention order must meet, where and within what time an objection must be filed, the alternatives that make release possible, and the authorities that can be approached when ordinary remedies fail.

1. The three basic conditions for detention

Under Article 100 of the Turkish Code of Criminal Procedure, a detention order may be issued only if three elements are present together:

  1. Concrete evidence showing a strong suspicion that an offence has been committed,
  2. A ground for detention, such as a risk of absconding, hiding or tampering with evidence,
  3. Detention being proportionate to the importance of the case and the expected sentence.

The law accepts that a ground for detention may be presumed for certain offences such as intentional killing, drug trafficking, robbery and aggravated sexual assault. Even for these "catalogue offences", however, the strong suspicion must be based on concrete evidence. Moreover, detention may not be ordered for offences punishable only by a judicial fine, or for offences whose maximum prison sentence does not exceed two years, except for intentional offences against bodily integrity.

2. An unreasoned detention order is unlawful

Under Article 101(2) of the Code, every decision ordering detention, extending detention or rejecting a request for release must clearly set out, with concrete facts, the evidence showing the strong suspicion, the existence of grounds for detention, the proportionality of the measure and why judicial control would be insufficient. Decisions that merely repeat the wording of the law or rely on stock phrases such as "the nature of the offence and the current state of evidence" do not meet this obligation.

The Constitutional Court has also emphasised in many judgments on the right to liberty and security that the reasoning for detention must be specific to the case. An effective objection should therefore target the gaps in the reasoning of the decision before turning to the suspect's personal circumstances.

3. Where and when is the objection filed?

During the investigation stage, an objection against a detention order issued by a criminal judgeship of peace is examined, under Article 268(3)(b), by the judge of the criminal court of first instance within the same jurisdiction. During the prosecution stage, objections against detention decisions of a criminal court of first instance are examined by the high criminal court, and objections against decisions of a high criminal court are examined by the next high criminal court in numerical order.

Following the amendment made by Law No. 7499 in 2024, the time limit for objection was extended from seven days to two weeks from the date the decision is learned. Yet every day spent in detention is a day of liberty lost. The objection should therefore not be left until the end of the period but filed as soon as possible after notification, supported by concrete evidence. It is made by a petition submitted to the judgeship or court that issued the decision; if that authority finds the objection justified, it may correct its own decision.

4. Release may be requested at any stage

Even if the objection is rejected, the road does not close. Under Article 104, the suspect or defendant may request release at any stage of the investigation and prosecution. In addition, during the investigation the criminal judgeship of peace must review whether continued detention is necessary at intervals of no more than thirty days (Article 108). During the prosecution stage, the court carries out this assessment at every hearing.

Developments over time, such as the collection of evidence, the hearing of witnesses or the receipt of an expert report, may remove the grounds for detention that were initially accepted. Petitions that do not rely on any new development and merely repeat earlier requests generally do not succeed.

5. The alternative to detention: judicial control

The legislator treats detention as a last resort. Article 109 allows the suspect to be placed under judicial control even where grounds for detention exist. A travel ban, reporting to a police station at regular intervals, not leaving a certain area or one's home, not going to certain places and depositing a security are the main measures. Every two days spent under the obligation not to leave one's home are credited as one day against any eventual sentence.

Showing which judicial control measure would serve the purpose of detention in the specific case significantly increases the chances of an objection or release request being granted. A stable residence, regular employment, family ties, a clean criminal record and the fact that most of the evidence has already been collected carry weight in this assessment.

6. Statutory maximum periods of detention

Article 102 sets firm limits on the length of detention:

  • In cases outside the jurisdiction of the high criminal court, at most one year, extendable by six months where necessary.
  • In cases within the jurisdiction of the high criminal court, at most two years; with extensions the total may not exceed three years, or five years for certain terrorism and state security offences.
  • During the investigation stage, detention may not exceed one year in high criminal court cases and six months in other cases.
  • For children under fifteen at the time of the offence these periods apply at one half, and for those under eighteen at three quarters.

Once these periods expire, release is mandatory, although judicial control may be imposed on the released person. In practice, release may also be requested well before the maximum periods by arguing that detention has exceeded a reasonable time and lost its proportionality.

7. When ordinary remedies are exhausted

After the objection remedies concerning detention have been exhausted, an individual application may be lodged with the Constitutional Court alleging a violation of the right to liberty and security. The application must be made within thirty days of the exhaustion of remedies. If that route also fails, an application to the European Court of Human Rights is possible.

If the detained person is later released in a file that ends with a decision of non-prosecution or acquittal, they may claim pecuniary and non-pecuniary compensation for unjust detention under Article 141 et seq. of the Code. The claim must be filed within three months of being notified that the decision has become final and in any event within one year of the date it became final.

8. Conclusion

A detention order is not final and unchangeable. The gaps in its reasoning, the principle of proportionality, judicial control alternatives and new developments in the file open the way to release when presented at the right time and to the right authority. Time is the most valuable factor in this process; when and with what evidence an objection is made determines the outcome as much as its content.
