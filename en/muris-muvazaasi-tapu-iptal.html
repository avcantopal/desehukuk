Depriving Heirs of Property: Challenging Transfers Disguised as Sales (Muris Muvazaası)

21 April 2026

A situation frequently encountered in the division of an estate is this: while still alive, a mother or father transferred a property to one of their children, registered at the land registry as a "sale", yet no real purchase price was ever paid. The other heirs discover after the death that a significant part of the estate changed hands years earlier. Known in Turkish law as "muris muvazaası" (simulated transfer by the deceased), this situation can lead to the cancellation of the title deed and the registration of the property in the heirs' names when brought before the court with appropriate evidence.

In this article we examine the legal basis of this doctrine, the facts courts look at, who may bring the case and under what conditions, and how it differs from an action for reduction (tenkis).

1. What is a simulated transfer by the deceased?

It occurs when the deceased, intending to deprive their heirs of property, transfers real estate that they actually wished to donate through a different transaction at the land registry, such as a sale or a contract of lifelong maintenance. On the surface there is a sale; the true intention of the parties, however, is a gratuitous transfer.

The most common examples are a property being shown as sold to only one or some of the children at a price far below its value, or being transferred to a second spouse or children from that marriage.

2. Legal basis: the 1974 Decision on the Unification of Case Law

The fundamental basis of these cases is the Court of Cassation's Decision on the Unification of Case Law dated 01.04.1974, numbered 1/2. According to this decision, a transfer disguised as a sale in order to deprive heirs of property contains two separate transactions. The apparent sale, which the parties did not really intend, is invalid because it is simulated. The concealed donation, which they did intend, is not valid because it was made without the official form required by law for donations of real estate. As a result, the transfer is legally invalid and the heirs may request the cancellation of the title deed.

3. The decisive element: the true intention of the deceased

The key question that determines the outcome is whether the deceased intended to deprive the heirs of property when making the transfer. In its decision of 02.11.2017 (Docket 2015/4642, Decision 2017/6120), the 1st Civil Chamber of the Court of Cassation reversed a judgment in favour of the claimants in a case where the deceased had concluded a lifelong maintenance contract with their son shortly before the transfer and the transfer was made to secure their care, holding that there was no intention to deprive the heirs. The decision emphasised that the purchase price need not necessarily be money and may consist of a service or labour.

This decision clearly shows that two transfers that look identical may lead to completely different outcomes depending on the purpose of the deceased.

4. What facts does the court look at?

Since the intention of the deceased cannot be known directly, courts infer it from the facts in the file. The main elements assessed in practice are:

  • The difference between the price shown at the land registry and the actual value of the property at the date of transfer,
  • Whether the buyer had the financial means to pay that price at the date of transfer,
  • Whether the deceased had a reasonable need to sell the property,
  • Where the money allegedly obtained from the sale was spent,
  • The relationship between the deceased and the heirs, estrangements and family developments,
  • Whether the transfer was intended to balance gifts previously made to other heirs.

None of these facts is sufficient on its own. The case is won by presenting witness statements, bank records, expert valuation and family documents as a whole.

5. Who may bring the case, and when?

The case is brought after the death of the deceased. Each heir may file it alone, without waiting for the other heirs to join, in proportion to their share of the inheritance. It is available to all statutory heirs, whether or not their reserved share has been infringed.

A title deed cancellation and registration case based on this doctrine is not subject to any limitation or strict time limit. However, as time passes witnesses' memories fade, documents become harder to obtain and the property may be transferred to third parties. The competent court is the civil court of first instance at the place where the property is located.

6. If the property has been sold to a third party

If the heir who acquired the property through the simulated transfer later sold it to a third party acting in good faith, the acquisition of that third party, who relied on the land registry, is protected. In that case the other heirs cannot recover the property but may claim compensation from the heir who benefited from the simulated transfer. Requesting an interim injunction on the property when filing the case is therefore critical to prevent a new transfer during the proceedings.

7. Difference from an action for reduction

This doctrine is often confused with an action for reduction (tenkis). An action for reduction aims to reduce dispositions of the deceased that infringe the reserved shares to the extent necessary to cover those shares, and is subject to a one-year period from discovery and in any event ten years. In a simulated transfer case, by contrast, it is argued that the transfer is wholly invalid and the heir requests registration in proportion to their statutory share.

In practice, asserting both claims in the same case, in a stated order of priority, is a frequently used method. If the simulation cannot be proven, the court examines the reduction claim. This structure reduces the risk of losing the case entirely.

8. Conclusion

The word "sale" at the land registry does not mean, in the eyes of the court, that a transfer was really a sale. What matters is whether the deceased intended to deprive the heirs of property, and that intention can only be demonstrated with a properly constructed body of evidence. Filing the case in time, obtaining an injunction on the property and ordering the claims correctly are the steps that directly determine the outcome.
