Drug Trafficking (TCC Art. 188) vs. Possession for Personal Use (TCC Art. 191): Court of Cassation Criteria

9 February 2026

In a file where drugs have been seized, the fate of the defence is often decided by a single question: did the suspect possess the substance for their own use, or to supply it to others? The answer to this question is the difference between an offence punishable by two to five years' imprisonment, which may end with deferral of the public prosecution, and an offence carrying a prison sentence of no less than ten years.

1. The legal framework of the two offences

Article 188(3) of the Turkish Criminal Code punishes a person who, without a licence, sells, offers for sale, supplies to others, dispatches, transports, stores, buys, accepts or possesses narcotic or stimulant substances within the country with imprisonment of no less than ten years and a judicial fine of one thousand to twenty thousand days. Under the fourth paragraph, the penalty is increased by half if the substance is heroin, cocaine, morphine, base morphine, synthetic cannabinoids and their derivatives, synthetic cathinones and their derivatives, synthetic opioids and their derivatives, or amphetamine and its derivatives.

Article 191, by contrast, punishes a person who buys, accepts or possesses narcotic or stimulant substances for personal use, or who uses them, with imprisonment of two to five years. For this offence, a five-year deferral of the public prosecution must be ordered at the investigation stage without seeking the conditions in Article 171 of the Code of Criminal Procedure, and a probation measure of at least one year is applied during the deferral period.

There is a notable overlap between the alternative acts in the two articles: "buying", "accepting" and "possessing" appear in both offences. What moves the act from one to the other is not the act itself, but the purpose for which it was carried out.

2. The decisive criterion: the purpose of possession

According to the case law of the Court of Cassation, the factor that plays a decisive role in determining whether possession of drugs constitutes possession for personal use or drug trafficking is the purpose of possession. In determining this purpose, the following criteria are considered together in each case:

  • whether the suspect engaged in any conduct aimed at selling, transferring or supplying the substance to others,
  • the place where and the manner in which the substance was kept,
  • the type and quantity of the substance.

As can be seen, the case law does not accept a conclusion based on a single criterion, for example only the weight in grams. None of these criteria is decisive on its own; the court determines the nature of the offence according to the conclusion reached when all the facts in the file are assessed together.

3. Facts treated in practice as evidence of intent to trade

a) A quantity exceeding the limit for personal use

Although the law contains no gram threshold, the concept of "a quantity exceeding the limit for personal use" is consistently used in the Court of Cassation's case law. Possession that clearly exceeds what a user could consume within a reasonable time is regarded as a fact inconsistent with personal use. For example, in a case where a quantity exceeding the personal-use limit was seized from the defendant in a transparent bag, characterising the act as trafficking was found to be correct.

Quantity must, however, also be handled carefully from the defence perspective. The personal-use limit varies according to the type of substance, its purity and the person's consumption habits. Whether the substance was weighed gross or net in the forensic report and whether its purity was determined are the first points to question in any assessment based on quantity.

b) Variety of substances

The seizure of more than one type of drug from a single person (for example heroin, cocaine and methamphetamine together) is accepted in case law as one of the facts pointing to a purpose of sale. A variety that is difficult to explain by a user profile is regarded by the courts as an indication of intent to trade.

c) Packaging and precision scales

This is the most frequently encountered and most heavily weighted group of evidence in practice. The substance being divided into small portions ready for sale and packaged separately, and the seizure at home or on the person of precision scales bearing drug residue and packaging materials (zip-lock bags, cling film, etc.), are facts that, according to the case law, support the conclusion that the substance was held for sale. In particular, the detection on the scales of residue of the same type as the seized substance significantly increases the weight of this evidence.

d) Communication records and witness statements

Message contents, contact entries and call traffic identified in phone examinations are among the strongest bases for a trafficking allegation. Where message contents are "fully consistent" with the statements, convictions are upheld. In practice, contact entries under nicknames evoking drugs and conversations about quantity and price are also regarded as expressions linked to trafficking.

4. Critical points for the defence

a) The principle that doubt benefits the defendant

According to the case law of the Court of Cassation, the basic condition for punishing a defendant is that the offence is proven with a certainty that leaves no room for doubt. A conviction cannot be based on events and allegations whose occurrence is doubtful or not fully clarified, interpreted against the defendant. A criminal conviction must rest not on a possibility but on certain and clear proof.

This principle has a direct effect on determining the nature of the offence in drug files. In a recent case, the quantity seized from a defendant caught after a chase following intelligence, the defendant's consistent defence throughout the proceedings that "I bought it for my own use", and the fact that the defendant had also been prosecuted separately for use were assessed together. Taking into account that no drugs forming the subject of the offence had been seized from the witnesses who said they had bought drugs from the defendant, the court concluded that there was no certain and convincing evidence free from all doubt to justify a conviction for trafficking; the conviction was reversed and the defendant's release was ordered.

b) An unsupported statement is not enough on its own

In drug files, a significant part of the accusation rests on the statement of another suspect seeking to benefit from effective repentance, along the lines of "I bought it from him". According to the case law of the Court of Cassation, an abstract statement not supported by other evidence is not sufficient for a conviction free from all doubt. Whether the statement is corroborated by independent evidence such as message records, physical surveillance, call records, or seized drugs or money must be scrutinised carefully.

c) Being a user does not exclude trafficking

A common mistake by the defence is to assume that proving the defendant is a user automatically removes the trafficking accusation. According to the case law, the detection of drugs in a urine test and a decision to defer prosecution for use on that basis do not prevent the trafficking offence from being established with respect to an act of sale in the same incident. Being a user becomes meaningful only together with other facts supporting that the substance was held for that purpose.

d) The defence of "buying to use together"

The situation of a person who buys drugs on behalf of a group of friends and hands them over to the group is one of the most debated issues in practice. According to the case law, for this defence to be accepted it is important that the statements of the other members of the group throughout the proceedings confirm it. In a case where the other persons did not state that they would use the substance together with the defendant, while communication records were linked to a sale, handing the substance over to others constituted the trafficking offence. In such defences it is therefore decisive to ensure that the statements of the other group members are taken consistently from the very first stage.

e) Change of characterisation during prosecution and deferral of the announcement of the verdict

Under Article 191(8) of the Criminal Code, if it is understood during prosecution for drug trafficking that the offence falls exclusively within possession for personal use, an HAGB (deferral of the announcement of the verdict) decision is issued for the defendant under the provisions of that article. This shows that, for a defendant detained on a trafficking charge, a request to change the characterisation of the offence may alter not only the length of the sentence but the outcome of the judgment entirely.

5. Conclusion

The distinction between Articles 188 and 191 of the Criminal Code is made not by a gram threshold but by the totality of the facts revealing the purpose of possession. The defence must therefore question each fact separately, from the net quantity and purity in the forensic report to how the packaging and scales were identified, whether the communication records are really linked to sale, and whether incriminating statements are supported by independent evidence. The consistency of the statement given at the very beginning of the investigation often determines the outcome of the file.
